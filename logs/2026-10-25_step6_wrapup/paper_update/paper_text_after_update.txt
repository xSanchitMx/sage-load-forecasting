Real-Time Self-Adaptive Agentic Intelligence for Electricity Load Forecasting Under Dynamic Demand Shifts
Sanchit Mukherjee 23BCE5126
Abstract—Short-term electricity load forecasting is normally performed with a single model that is trained offline and then deployed unchanged, so it degrades silently when consumption shifts. This report presents SAGE (Self-Adaptive aGEntic forecaster), a real-time, self-adaptive agentic system that removes this failure mode. A pool of five inexpensive forecasters (seasonal-naive, Holt–Winters, seasonal ARIMA, gradient-boosted trees and an online linear learner) is combined online by MELO, a multi-horizon aggregation method. An agentic layer monitors the residual of the served forecast with ADWIN and the Page–Hinkley test, refits the batch models immediately and at follow-up times after each alarm, and uses a contextual serving agent—a full-information contextual bandit—to re-weight the combiner and the individual models every hour. Evaluated prequentially on eight clients of the UCI ElectricityLoadDiagrams20112014 dataset, with every setting chosen on validation data, SAGE reaches 4.70% test MAPE, the primary metric, against 4.82% for MELO and 5.01% for the initial rule-based controller, and it is significantly more accurate than MELO under Diebold–Mariano tests. Under four injected demand shifts it lowers MAPE and CV-RMSE relative to MELO in every scenario and matches daily refitting with about a fifth of the refits, at roughly 31 ms per hourly step on a laptop CPU. The contribution is an integrated, reproducible adaptive forecasting system and a controlled evaluation of each of its parts, rather than a new learning algorithm.
Index Terms—short-term load forecasting, concept drift, online learning, forecast combination, contextual bandit, adaptive ensembles, agentic systems.
I. Introduction
Accurate short-term electricity load forecasting (STLF) underpins unit commitment, economic dispatch, reserve sizing and market bidding, and small percentage improvements in forecast accuracy translate into substantial operational savings. Decades of research have produced a large catalogue of STLF methods—statistical, machine-learning and deep-learning—but the dominant deployment pattern remains the same: a single model is selected and tuned on historical data, then run unchanged in production.
This pattern fails under dynamic demand shifts. Electricity consumption is non-stationary: its level, its daily and weekly shape, and its sensitivity to weather all change over time because of seasonal transitions, extreme-weather episodes, infrastructure changes and behavioural shifts. A model fitted to one regime loses accuracy when the regime changes—a phenomenon known as concept drift [7]—and, because the pipeline has no mechanism to notice or react, the loss persists until it is caught manually.
Recent research offers partial remedies. Reinforcement-learning agents can re-select or re-combine models online [2], [4]; drift detectors can flag distributional change [7], [11]; detect-then-adapt schemes retrain a model once drift is confirmed [9], [10]; online ensembles reweight their members by recent performance [14], [15], [16]; and continual-learning frameworks keep a single model current over long horizons [17], [18]. Large-language-model agents have also been proposed as forecasting controllers [5], [6]. However, these lines of work remain largely separate: the agentic controllers are select-only or rely on heavy deep reinforcement learning or on LLM inference; the drift work adapts a single model’s parameters rather than managing a pool; and the online ensembles lack an explicit drift signal gating their more expensive actions.
This report specifies a system that integrates these elements into a single, deliberately lightweight controller and evaluates it under a realistic streaming protocol. Its contributions are:
An integrated self-adaptive architecture that manages a heterogeneous pool of inexpensive forecasters—rather than adapting one network—by coupling an online combiner (MELO [16]) to an explicit statistical drift detector whose alarms trigger immediate and follow-up refits.
A contextual serving agent, a full-information contextual bandit over the combiner and the individual models, which replaces the initial two-arm bandit controller.
Drift detection performed on the residual of the served (deployed) forecast rather than on any individual model, with the resulting blind spot stated explicitly.
An evaluation under a realistic streaming protocol that includes four online aggregation baselines and MELO, a daily-refitting reference, four injected demand-shift scenarios, and Diebold–Mariano significance tests.

II. Literature Review
Research relevant to a self-adaptive agentic load-forecasting system spans five areas: (A) learned and agentic control of forecasting, (B) concept-drift detection and online model adaptation, (C) online and adaptive ensembles, (D) continual learning for energy forecasting, and (E) forecasting models, benchmarks and surveys. Each is discussed below; Table I gives a structured summary of the twenty works.
A. Learned and Agentic Control of Forecasting
A growing body of work reframes forecasting from a static, single-pass mapping into a process actively managed by an agent. Cheng et al. [1] cast forecasting as an agentic loop of perception, planning, action, reflection and memory, naming agentic reinforcement learning as one way to realise it. The seminal concrete instance for load forecasting is Feng and Zhang [2], in which a tabular Q-learning agent selects, at each step, the best model from a pool of ten, reducing error by roughly 50% over any fixed model; its action space is select-only, the policy is trained offline, and no explicit drift signal is used. Fu et al. [4] (RLMC) generalise selection to continuous combination weights via a deep actor–critic. Wu, Ma and Aliev [3] cast step-by-step load and price forecasting as a Markov decision process solved with a Deep Q-Network, with the agent emitting the forecast directly. Two further works place a large language model at the centre of the loop: Nexus [5] and the interactive system of Zuo et al. [6], both of which incur LLM inference cost and latency that make a per-hour decision over long streams impractical. This area establishes the value of an agent that manages forecasting, but the available agents are either restricted to model selection [2], reliant on heavy deep reinforcement learning [3], [4], or built on LLMs [5], [6].
B. Concept-Drift Detection and Online Model Adaptation
Gama et al. [7] provide the authoritative taxonomy of concept drift and codify the prequential evaluation protocol used throughout streaming learning. In load forecasting specifically, Jagait et al. [8] combine an online adaptive recurrent network with a rolling ARIMA corrector. Zhang et al. [9] (D3A) formalise a detect-then-adapt loop; Zhao and Shen [10] (Proceed) make adaptation proactive; Lu et al. [11] prove that an adaptive detection threshold dominates any fixed one. Hu et al. [12] (AdaCNP) address extreme-weather load with a conditional neural process, and Zhu et al. [13] (Dynamic TMoE) drive a mixture-of-experts with drift detection, instantiating and pruning experts online. This area supplies mature detectors and single-model or mixture-of-experts adaptation, but the detector is seldom tied to a discrete, cost-aware controller acting over a heterogeneous pool.
C. Online and Adaptive Ensembles
Kolter and Maloof [14] (Dynamic Weighted Majority) established the template for managing a set of predictors online via performance-weighted updates and add/remove of members. Zhang et al. [15] (OneNet) apply online ensembling to forecasting, and Wang, Goude and Yao [16] (MELO) hedge adaptation experts across forgetting horizons with tracking-regret guarantees. These works show performance-weighted combination adapts well to non-stationarity, but none couples the ensemble to an explicit drift detector gating its structural actions, nor to a learned decision about whether to rely on the ensemble at all.
D. Continual Learning for Energy Forecasting
He, Uhrweiller and Sick [17] frame long-running power forecasting as continual learning for regression on a single evolving model, and Karaouli et al. [18] study catastrophic forgetting when time-series foundation models are continually fine-tuned. This area addresses sustained self-adaptation but, again, for one model rather than a managed pool.
E. Forecasting Models, Benchmarks and Surveys
Shao et al. [19] (BasicTS+) provide a reproducible benchmarking pipeline over 18 datasets and show that the best model class is dataset-dependent, and Hong et al. [20] give a field-level review of energy forecasting. Together these fix the datasets, baselines and evaluation discipline adopted here and confirm the premise underlying a model pool: no single forecaster dominates across regimes.
F. Research Gap
Across the twenty works reviewed, every ingredient of an adaptive forecasting system exists in isolation—agentic or learned control [1], [2], [4], drift detection [7], [9], [10], [11], [13], performance-driven ensembling [14], [15], [16], and continual adaptation [17], [18]—but no single work combines them. Specifically, there is no system that simultaneously (i) manages a heterogeneous pool of inexpensive models rather than adapting one network, (ii) couples an explicit statistical drift detector to a discrete, cost-aware controller action set spanning selection, blending, demotion, reinstatement and retraining, (iii) monitors the residual of the deployed (served) forecast, and (iv) is light enough to take a decision every hour over a multi-year stream without a GPU or an LLM. The proposed methodology targets this gap, and additionally asks whether a lightweight learned meta-policy measurably improves on a transparent deterministic rule set for this task.
III. Proposed Methodology
This section presents the proposed methodology: a real-time, self-adaptive agentic forecasting system for short-term electricity load prediction under dynamic demand shifts.

Conventional pipelines commit to a single model trained offline and then deployed unchanged; such a model degrades silently whenever the underlying consumption process shifts, and no corrective action is taken until the degradation is noticed manually [7]. The system proposed here removes that failure mode by maintaining a pool of lightweight, complementary forecasting models and placing an autonomous controller above the pool. At every time step the controller observes the incoming load measurement and the forecasting error it produced, tests that error stream for concept drift, and decides—from streaming feedback rather than a fixed rule—how much to trust the combined forecast versus each individual model, and when to refit.
The system is organised as a closed monitor–detect–decide–act–adapt loop of five cooperating modules. Module 1 (Data Acquisition and Preprocessing) converts the raw dataset into a clean, causally scaled, feature-augmented hourly stream. Module 2 (Forecasting Model Pool) maintains  cheap-to-retrain models that each emit a one-step-ahead forecast. Module 3 (Concept Drift Detection) applies two statistical detectors to the residual stream of the served forecast. Module 4 (Adaptive Agentic Controller) fuses the pool’s forecasts with the MELO combiner, refits the batch models after a drift alarm, and re-weights the combiner and the individual models every hour through a contextual serving agent. Module 5 (Evaluation and Reward Engine) scores every served forecast, computes the accuracy, regret and adaptation-speed metrics, and returns every candidate’s loss to the agent. Shift experiment and significance. To test adaptation under known changes, four demand shifts are injected into each client’s test series at 12 and 33 weeks: level (×1.3), ramp (to ×1.3 over four weeks), daily amplitude (×1.6) and a one-week shock (×1.4). The second event reverses the first, except for the shock, which repeats. Differences between methods are tested with the Diebold–Mariano test [30] on hourly losses (Newey–West variance, 24-h lag) for every client and scenario, and with a Wilcoxon signed-rank test across the 40 resulting series.

A. Experimental Setup
All experiments are implemented in Python and run on a laptop CPU with no GPU: Holt–Winters and SARIMA use statsmodels, the gradient-boosted member uses LightGBM, and the online learner and both detectors use river. Every method is evaluated under the same prequential protocol on the test block (9,207 hours per client), and every setting—the drift thresholds, MELO’s inflation scale, the alarm response, and the agent’s  and —was chosen on the validation block, including validation copies of the shift scenarios, before the test block was run once. The compared methods are the five single models; a validation-chosen single model; an equal-weight ensemble; four online aggregation baselines, Follow-the-Leader and Hedge [29], [31], Dynamic Weighted Majority [14] and MLpol [28]; MELO [16], replayed over the same pool forecasts with weekly refits; the initial controller (top-3 inverse-error blend with a Discounted-UCB1 bandit [26], [27]); MELO with daily refits under the shift scenarios; and the proposed system, SAGE. MAPE is the primary accuracy metric, because it is scale-free and so weights small and large clients equally; CV-RMSE is reported alongside it because MAPE is inflated in hours with near-zero load.
B. Dataset Description
Experiments use the UCI ElectricityLoadDiagrams20112014 dataset, a public benchmark of individual electricity-consumption series. The raw data provide 15-minute readings for 370 clients over 2011–2015; following common practice it is aggregated to hourly resolution and restricted to the 321 clients with complete history over 2012–2014, giving 26,304 hourly observations per client. A stratified subset of eight clients is selected by ranking clients on the coefficient of variation of their consumption and sampling across the low-, medium- and high-variability terciles, so the evaluation spans both stable and volatile demand. Calendar covariates (hour-of-day, day-of-week, month, holiday flag) are attached to every timestamp and each series is causally standardised as in (2).
C. Accuracy on the Real Data
Table II and Fig. 3 compare all methods on the real test data. SAGE has the lowest MAPE (4.70%) and the lowest CV-RMSE (6.34%) of every method: MELO reaches 4.82% and 6.47%, the best other online aggregation method, DWM, 4.98% MAPE, the best single model chosen on validation 5.19%, and the equal-weight ensemble 6.14%. Its MAE is slightly above MELO’s, because the agent leans on the individual models in low-load hours, where percentage errors are largest. Every online aggregation method beats the initial top-3 blend, which is why MELO replaced it as the combiner.

Fig. 3. Test MAPE by method (mean of eight clients); blue: SAGE, orange: MELO.
Fig. 4 compares SAGE with the four strongest baselines client by client. SAGE has lower CV-RMSE than Hedge, DWM and MLpol on every client and lower MAPE on seven of the eight (on MT_269 DWM is 0.001 point ahead); it beats MELO on MAPE on 4 of the eight. On the four most regular clients it is within 0.1 point of MELO, with MELO marginally ahead; its large gains come on the two irregular clients examined in Section IV-E.

D. Adaptation Under Injected Demand Shifts
Table III and Fig. 5 report the shift experiment. SAGE has lower MAPE and lower CV-RMSE than MELO in every scenario, while Hedge, DWM and MLpol are worse than MELO in every scenario. SAGE also has lower MAPE than MELO refitted every day, in every scenario, while refitting about five times less often; on CV-RMSE the two are level. The detector caught 10 of 16 level shifts and 14 of 16 temporary shocks (median delays 25 h and 30 h) but only 5 of 16 slow ramps and 9 of 16 amplitude changes, with about 0.2 false alarms per week. After temporary shocks SAGE recovered in a median 133 h against 149 h for MELO, but after level shifts it was slower (138 h against 114 h). In the amplitude scenario MT_135’s near-zero hours inflate MAPE, so CV-RMSE is the fairer measure there.

E. Where SAGE Helps Most
The largest gains over MELO come from two real clients. MT_105 (Fig. 7) is the largest CV-RMSE gain (5.79% vs 6.93%). After four quiet weeks at about 100 kW, its load jumped about fivefold on 23 October 2014. SARIMA tracked the new level, and the agent moved almost all of its weight onto SARIMA within five hours; the detector fired 7 h and 15 h after the jump, and the refits it triggered included the new data. In the weekly-refit pool used by MELO, SARIMA fell back to the old level nine hours after the jump, and MELO’s correction experts, which can extrapolate beyond the pool, overshot: at that hour it forecast 1,993 kW against an actual 824 kW. That single hour accounts for 84% of the difference in squared error on this client; without it SAGE is still better (5.80% vs 5.99%).

Fig. 7. MT_105, real data: a fivefold load jump on 23 October 2014. Load and forecasts (top) and SAGE’s agent weights (bottom, 6-h mean). Solid line: the jump; dashed: drift alarms.
MT_135 (Fig. 8) is the largest MAPE gain (11.53% vs 12.48%, and 7–9% lower in every shift scenario). Its load profile is inverted: it peaks at night at about 680 kW and falls to 150–300 kW by day. In the quietest quarter of hours MELO over-forecasts by 13 kW on average, which is a large percentage of a small load; the agent learns that in these hours the seasonal-naive forecast (the same hour one week earlier) is more reliable and raises its weight there from 0.16 to 0.30, and cuts the bias to 9 kW and the MAPE of those hours from 17.7% to 14.1%. The other large gains are at the morning and evening transitions (08:00, 19:00–21:00), where it leans on LightGBM. This client supplies most of SAGE’s mean MAPE gain over MELO.

F. Statistical Significance and Cost
Table IV summarises the Diebold–Mariano tests of SAGE against MELO. SAGE is significantly better overall on absolute, squared and percentage error (Wilcoxon p = 0.01–0.04 across the 40 series) and significantly better in 17 series on absolute and percentage error, but significantly worse in 5–8. In an ablation without the contextual agent, the drift response alone gives the same CV-RMSE gain and about 0.1 point higher MAPE; the agent’s own contribution is not significant (p = 0.25–0.33).

SAGE refits its batch models about 4.3 times per week, against 3.0 for MELO with weekly refits and 21 with daily refits, and runs at about 31 ms per hourly step on a laptop CPU, of which the agentic logic—drift detection, MELO and the agent—is 1.9%.
G. What Did Not Work
Several designs were tested on validation data and rejected: the initial top-3 inverse-error blend, which every online aggregation method beat; refitting on post-change data only after an alarm; resetting MELO’s regrets and covariances on alarms; and training the agent on squared rather than absolute loss. The evaluation also exposed four faults in the first implementation—a seasonal-phase error in Holt–Winters, SARIMA forecasting up to 168 h ahead without new observations, a drift detector that fired on a fixed weekly timer, and a recovery metric that always returned one hour—which inflated the earlier error figures and were corrected before every result reported here.
V. Conclusion
This work built SAGE, an hourly, GPU-free adaptive forecasting system for client-level electricity load: a pool of five inexpensive forecasters combined by MELO, an explicit drift detector whose alarms trigger immediate and follow-up refits, and a contextual agent that re-weights the combiner and the models each hour. On eight UCI clients it has the lowest MAPE and CV-RMSE of all methods tested and is significantly more accurate than MELO, the strongest online aggregation baseline; under injected demand shifts it beats daily refitting on MAPE and matches it on CV-RMSE at a fifth of the retraining cost. Its largest gains come from sudden real level changes and irregular load profiles; on regular clients it matches MELO. Richer context such as weather and calendar events, and giving the agent control over refit timing, are natural next steps.
